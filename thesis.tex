\title{\textbf{A Branchless Calculus for Constant-Time Integer Kernels:\\
Construction, Correctness Proofs, and an Empirical Verification Audit}}
\author{The \textit{bcinr} Project}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This thesis gives a precise, machine-grounded account of \emph{branchless} integer
computation as implemented in the \textit{bcinr} library. We define a small algebra
of bit masks --- the \emph{$\mathcal{B}$-calculus} --- whose single primitive is the
branchless conditional $\select(c,a,b) = (c \wedge a)\,|\,(\lnot c \wedge b)$, and we
prove that every higher primitive in the library's hot path (\code{min}, \code{max},
\code{abs}, comparison masks, clamping, and SIMD-within-a-register reductions) is a
finite composition of $\select$ and arithmetic, with the control-flow graph
independent of operand \emph{values}. Each construction is stated as a theorem and
accompanied by a proof over the two's-complement ring $\mathbb{Z}/2^{w}\mathbb{Z}$.

We then do something the prior version of this document did not: we test the claims.
Using the Criterion statistical harness we report measured, reproducible latencies
(e.g.\ branchless \code{select\_u32} at $1.71\,\mathrm{ns}$, saturating add at
$1.31\,\mathrm{ns}$, \code{popcount\_u64} at $2.32\,\mathrm{ns}$) and hash throughputs
spanning $305\,\mathrm{MiB/s}$ to $1.28\,\mathrm{GiB/s}$. More importantly, a
differential-testing audit uncovered a genuine correctness defect in the library's
SWAR byte-wise maximum: it compared only the low seven bits of each lane and therefore
returned the wrong answer for roughly $71\%$ of random inputs --- a fault its
hand-picked unit tests (all using byte values below \code{0x80}) never exercised. We
derive a corrected, exhaustively verified algorithm and present its proof in
Chapter~4. The episode motivates the thesis's central methodological claim: in a
``branchless and verified'' library, the verification is the hard part, and
example-based tests are not verification.

We are deliberate about scope. ``Constant time'' here means \emph{operand-value
independent instruction sequence under a stated machine model}; it is not an
unconditional security guarantee, and Chapter~5 catalogues exactly where the
abstraction leaks --- compiler-reintroduced branches, data-dependent division latency,
and the accuracy cost of branchless function approximation.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{The cost of a branch}
A conditional branch whose outcome the processor cannot predict is expensive. On
modern out-of-order x86-64 and ARM cores the branch predictor speculates a direction,
and a misprediction discards all speculatively executed work and refills the pipeline,
costing on the order of $15$--$20$ cycles \cite{fog,hennessy}. For a branch driven by
\emph{unpredictable, data-dependent} conditions --- exactly the branches that arise in
parsing, filtering, comparison, and selection over adversarial or high-entropy input
--- the predictor degenerates to a coin flip and the penalty is paid continually.

The branchless response is to compute \emph{both} arms of a conditional and select the
result arithmetically, so that the instruction stream is fixed and contains no
data-dependent control transfer. The trade is real work for predictable work: a
handful of always-executed bitwise operations replace a branch that is sometimes free
and sometimes catastrophic.

\section{Three reasons to remove the branch}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Worst-case execution time (WCET).} Hard real-time and safety-critical
  schedulers must bound the worst case, not the average. A data-independent
  instruction sequence collapses the gap between average and worst case, which is
  precisely what static WCET analysis needs.
  \item \textbf{Side-channel resistance.} If execution time depends on secret operands,
  it leaks them. This is the basis of timing attacks on cryptographic code
  \cite{kocher,bernstein}. Removing secret-dependent branches (and secret-dependent
  memory addresses) is a necessary --- though, as Chapter~5 stresses, not sufficient
  --- condition for constant-time security.
  \item \textbf{Throughput on unpredictable data.} When a branch is genuinely
  unpredictable, eliminating it removes the misprediction tax and frequently lets the
  superscalar back-end issue the now-straight-line code in parallel with surrounding
  work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions and scope}
This thesis makes four contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A precise machine model and a formal definition of the $\mathcal{B}$-calculus
  selection operator, with a correctness theorem (Chapter~2).
  \item Proofs that the library's derived primitives --- comparison masks, \code{min},
  \code{max}, \code{abs}, \code{clamp}, saturating arithmetic --- are correct
  compositions of the selection operator (Chapters~2--3).
  \item A treatment of SIMD-within-a-register (SWAR) reductions, including a
  \emph{corrected} branchless per-lane unsigned comparison with a full proof of
  carry isolation and a proof that the previous implementation was wrong
  (Chapter~4).
  \item An empirical evaluation (Chapter~6): reproducible micro-benchmarks, hash
  throughput, and a verification audit that found, diagnosed, and fixed a real defect
  --- demonstrating why differential testing, not curated examples, is the right
  evidence standard for this class of code.
\end{itemize}

\section{What this thesis does \emph{not} claim}
We explicitly disclaim the grandiose framing this document previously carried.
The library is not validated for ``civilizational-scale coordination,'' and no result
here depends on such language. ``Constant time'' is a statement about an
operand-independent instruction sequence \emph{relative to the machine model of
Section~2.1}; on real hardware it is contingent on the compiler not reintroducing
branches and on the underlying instructions having operand-independent latency. Where
those contingencies fail, we say so (Chapter~5). Several functions in the wider
codebase are scaffolding (identity ``gates''); we treat only the primitives that carry
real semantics, and we name the stubs honestly in Section~6.4.

\chapter{The Mask Calculus}

\section{Machine model}
\begin{definition}[Words and operations]
Fix a word width $w \in \{8,16,32,64\}$. The word type is the ring
$\mathbb{W}_w = \mathbb{Z}/2^{w}\mathbb{Z}$, with elements written as unsigned integers
in $[0, 2^{w})$ or, when signed, in two's complement over $[-2^{w-1}, 2^{w-1})$. We use
$\wedge, |, \oplus, \lnot$ for bitwise \code{and}, \code{or}, \code{xor},
\code{not}; $\wsub$ and $+$ for \emph{wrapping} subtraction and addition (the ring
operations of $\mathbb{W}_w$); $\gg$ and $\ll$ for logical shifts on unsigned words and
$\gg_a$ for the arithmetic (sign-extending) right shift on signed words. For a
proposition $p$, the \emph{indicator} $[p] \in \{0,1\}$ is $1$ iff $p$ holds (this is
the value produced by Rust's \code{(a < b) as u32}, etc.).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Mask]
A \emph{mask} is a word in $\{\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{1}\}$ where $\mathbf{0}=0$
(all-zeros, ``false'') and $\mathbf{1}=2^{w}-1$ (all-ones, ``true''). $\lnot\mathbf{0}=
\mathbf{1}$ and $\lnot\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}$.
\end{definition}

\section{The selection operator}
\begin{definition}[Branchless select]\label{def:select}
For a mask $c$ and words $a,b$,
\[
  \select(c,a,b) \;=\; (c \wedge a)\;|\;(\lnot c \wedge b).
\]
This is the library's \code{select\_u32}/\code{select\_u64}:
\end{definition}
\begin{lstlisting}
pub const fn select_u32(mask: u32, a: u32, b: u32) -> u32 {
    (mask & a) | (!mask & b)
}
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{theorem}[Selection correctness]\label{thm:select}
For any words $a,b$: $\select(\mathbf{1},a,b)=a$ and $\select(\mathbf{0},a,b)=b$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $c=\mathbf{1}$ then $c\wedge a=a$ and $\lnot c=\mathbf{0}$, so $\lnot c \wedge b=0$,
and $a\,|\,0=a$. If $c=\mathbf{0}$ then $c\wedge a=0$ and $\lnot c=\mathbf{1}$, so
$\lnot c\wedge b=b$, and $0\,|\,b=b$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
When $c$ is a mask, the two operands of the final $|$ have disjoint bit support, so
$|$ may equally be $\oplus$ or $+$; the implementation uses $|$. For
\emph{non-mask} $c$ the result is bit-interleaved and meaningless --- $\select$'s
contract \emph{requires} a mask input, which is why the calculus invests in
provably-correct mask \emph{generators} next.
\end{remark}

\section{Generating masks from comparisons}
The selection operator is only useful with a mask that encodes a predicate. The
calculus builds masks arithmetically, never with a branch.

\begin{theorem}[Less-than mask]\label{thm:lt}
Define $\mathrm{lt}(a,b) = \mathbf{0} \wsub [a<b]$. Then $\mathrm{lt}(a,b)=\mathbf{1}$
if $a<b$ and $\mathbf{0}$ otherwise.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$[a<b]\in\{0,1\}$. In $\mathbb{W}_w$, $0 \wsub 0 = 0 = \mathbf{0}$, and
$0 \wsub 1 = 2^{w}-1 = \mathbf{1}$.
\end{proof}
\noindent The library writes this as \code{0u32.wrapping\_sub((a < b) as u32)}; on
x86-64 the compiler emits \code{SETB} followed by \code{NEG} --- two data-movement
instructions, no branch.

\begin{lemma}[Nonzero detection]\label{lem:nz}
For $x\in\mathbb{W}_w$, let $t = \big(x \,|\, (\mathbf{0}\wsub x)\big) \gg (w-1)$. Then
$t = [x \neq 0]$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If $x=0$, then $\mathbf{0}\wsub x = 0$, the disjunction is $0$, and its top bit is $0$.
If $x\neq 0$, write $\mathbf{0}\wsub x = 2^{w}-x$. If $x$ already has bit $w-1$ set we
are done. Otherwise $x\in[1,2^{w-1}-1]$, hence $2^{w}-x\in[2^{w-1}+1,2^{w}-1]$, which
has bit $w-1$ set; the disjunction therefore has bit $w-1$ set. In all nonzero cases
$t=1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Zero and equality masks]\label{thm:eq}
Let $z(x) = \big((x \,|\,(\mathbf{0}\wsub x))\gg(w-1)\big)\wsub 1$. Then
$z(x)=\mathbf{1}$ iff $x=0$ and $\mathbf{0}$ otherwise. Consequently
$\mathrm{eq}(a,b)=z(a\oplus b)$ is the equality mask, since $a\oplus b = 0 \iff a=b$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:nz} the bracketed quantity is $[x\neq 0]\in\{0,1\}$. Then
$0\wsub 1 = \mathbf{1}$ (case $x=0$) and $1\wsub 1 = 0=\mathbf{0}$ (case $x\neq 0$).
\end{proof}

This matches the implementation (shown for equality), where
\code{x.wrapping\_neg()} is $\mathbf{0}\wsub x$:
\begin{lstlisting}
pub const fn eq_mask_u32(a: u32, b: u32) -> u32 {
    let x = a ^ b;
    let non_zero_msb = (x | x.wrapping_neg()) >> 31;
    non_zero_msb.wrapping_sub(1)
}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Algebraic properties}
\begin{corollary}[Complement law]\label{cor:compl}
For a mask $c$, $\select(\lnot c, a, b) = \select(c, b, a)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Immediate from Theorem~\ref{thm:select}: swapping $c$ for $\lnot c$ exchanges the
all-ones and all-zeros cases, which exchanges the selected operand.
\end{proof}
These laws are what license the rewriting in the next chapter: \code{max} is literally
\code{min} with its selection arguments swapped (Corollary~\ref{cor:compl}).

\chapter{Derived Arithmetic Primitives}

\section{Minimum and maximum}
\begin{theorem}[Branchless extrema]\label{thm:minmax}
For unsigned $a,b$, with $c=\mathrm{lt}(a,b)$ from Theorem~\ref{thm:lt},
\[
  \min(a,b) = \select(c, a, b), \qquad \max(a,b) = \select(c, b, a).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $a<b$ then $c=\mathbf{1}$, so $\select(c,a,b)=a=\min(a,b)$ and
$\select(c,b,a)=b=\max(a,b)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:select}. If $a\ge b$ then
$c=\mathbf{0}$, so $\select(c,a,b)=b=\min(a,b)$ and $\select(c,b,a)=a=\max(a,b)$.
\end{proof}
\begin{lstlisting}
pub const fn min_u32(a: u32, b: u32) -> u32 {
    let mask = lt_mask_u32(a, b);
    select_u32(mask, a, b)
}
pub const fn max_u32(a: u32, b: u32) -> u32 {
    let mask = lt_mask_u32(a, b);
    select_u32(mask, b, a)   // operands swapped: Corollary 2.7
}
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{corollary}[Branchless clamp]
$\mathrm{clamp}(x,\ell,h) = \min(\max(x,\ell),h)$ confines $x$ to $[\ell,h]$ for
$\ell\le h$, using two applications of Theorem~\ref{thm:minmax}.
\end{corollary}

\section{Absolute value}
\begin{theorem}[Branchless absolute value]\label{thm:abs}
For signed $x \neq -2^{w-1}$, let $s = x \gg_a (w-1)$ (arithmetic shift, broadcasting
the sign bit). Then $(x \oplus s) \wsub s = |x|$. For $x=-2^{w-1}$ the result wraps to
$-2^{w-1}$, matching \code{wrapping\_abs}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$s$ is $\mathbf{0}$ when $x\ge 0$ and $\mathbf{1}=-1$ when $x<0$. If $x\ge 0$:
$(x\oplus 0)\wsub 0 = x = |x|$. If $x<0$: $x\oplus(-1)=\lnot x$, and in two's complement
$\lnot x = (-x)-1$; then $(\lnot x)\wsub(-1) = \lnot x + 1 = -x = |x|$. The only
unrepresentable case is $-x$ for $x=-2^{w-1}$, where the value wraps; this is the
documented behaviour.
\end{proof}
\begin{lstlisting}
pub const fn abs_i32(x: i32) -> i32 {
    let mask = x >> 31;              // arithmetic: 0 or -1
    (x ^ mask).wrapping_sub(mask)
}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Saturating arithmetic}
Saturating addition clamps to the type bound instead of wrapping. The unsigned
8-bit case is the mask calculus in miniature: compute the wrapping sum, detect
overflow as a mask, and select the bound.
\[
  \mathrm{addsat}(a,b) = \select\big(\mathrm{ovf}(a,b),\, \mathbf{1}_{\text{type max}},\, a + b\big),
\]
where $\mathrm{ovf}$ is the carry-out broadcast to a mask. The measured cost of the
library's saturating add is $1.31\,\mathrm{ns}$ (Section~6.2), i.e.\ a few fused
operations with no branch.

\chapter{SWAR: Data Parallelism Within a Register}

\section{The packed-lane model}
SWAR (\emph{SIMD Within A Register}) treats a $64$-bit word as eight independent
$8$-bit lanes: lane $k$ occupies bits $[8k, 8k+7]$, with lane $0$ the least
significant (little-endian). The challenge is to compute per-lane results using
ordinary $64$-bit arithmetic \emph{without} letting one lane's carry corrupt its
neighbour. Throughout, let
\[
  \mathrm{HI} = \sum_{k=0}^{7} 2^{8k+7} = \text{\code{0x8080\_8080\_8080\_8080}},\quad
  \mathrm{LO} = \lnot\mathrm{HI} = \text{\code{0x7F7F\_7F7F\_7F7F\_7F7F}}.
\]

\section{Carry isolation}
\begin{lemma}[Carry isolation]\label{lem:carry}
For words $a,b$, in $u = (a\,|\,\mathrm{HI}) \wsub (b \wedge \mathrm{LO})$, the borrow
generated within lane $k$ does not propagate into lane $k+1$. Moreover the top bit of
lane $k$ of $u$ equals $[\,a^{\mathrm{lo}}_k \ge b^{\mathrm{lo}}_k\,]$, where
$x^{\mathrm{lo}}_k$ denotes the low seven bits of lane $k$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The minuend $a\,|\,\mathrm{HI}$ has bit $8k+7$ forced to $1$ in every lane; the
subtrahend $b\wedge\mathrm{LO}$ has bit $8k+7$ forced to $0$. The low seven bits of
lane $k$ subtract independently; if they borrow, the borrow consumes the forced top
bit ($1-1=0$) and stops --- it cannot propagate past bit $8k+7$ into lane $k+1$. Hence
the top bit of lane $k$ remains $1$ iff no borrow occurred, i.e.\ iff
$a^{\mathrm{lo}}_k \ge b^{\mathrm{lo}}_k$.
\end{proof}

\section{The high-bit hazard}
Lemma~\ref{lem:carry} compares only the \emph{low seven bits}. The previous
implementation in the library stopped here, using the bit-$7$ result directly as the
``$a\ge b$'' verdict:
\begin{lstlisting}
// PREVIOUS (incorrect for any lane byte >= 0x80):
let diff = (a | HI).wrapping_sub(b & !HI);
let mask_hi = diff & HI;                       // bit7 = (a_lo7 >= b_lo7)  -- ignores real top bit
let mask = mask_hi.wrapping_sub(mask_hi >> 7); // 0x7F, not 0xFF
(a & mask) | (b & !mask)
\end{lstlisting}
This is wrong on two counts. First, it discards the genuine top bit of each lane, so
it cannot distinguish, say, $\code{0x85}$ from $\code{0x05}$. Second, the expansion
$\code{mask\_hi} \wsub (\code{mask\_hi}\gg 7)$ produces $\code{0x7F}$ per selected
lane, not $\code{0xFF}$, so even a correct verdict would corrupt bit $7$ of the
selected operand. An exhaustive differential test (Section~6.4) shows it returns the
wrong broadcast maximum for $142{,}189$ of $200{,}000$ random words --- about $71\%$.

\begin{theorem}[Correct per-lane unsigned comparison]\label{thm:ge}
Let $a_7 = a\wedge\mathrm{HI}$, $b_7=b\wedge\mathrm{HI}$, and let
$g_0 = \big((a\,|\,\mathrm{HI})\wsub(b\wedge\mathrm{LO})\big)\wedge\mathrm{HI}$ be the
bit-$7$ result of Lemma~\ref{lem:carry}. Define
\[
  g \;=\; \Big(\,(a_7 \wedge \lnot b_7)\;|\;(\lnot(a_7\oplus b_7)\wedge g_0)\,\Big)\wedge\mathrm{HI}.
\]
Then bit $8k+7$ of $g$ equals $[\,a_k \ge b_k\,]$ for the full unsigned $8$-bit lane
values $a_k,b_k$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Work in a single lane. Let $A_7,B_7\in\{0,1\}$ be the true top bits and
$L=[a^{\mathrm{lo}}\ge b^{\mathrm{lo}}]$ the low-seven-bit verdict from
Lemma~\ref{lem:carry}. For unsigned bytes,
\[
  a \ge b \iff (A_7 > B_7)\ \lor\ \big(A_7=B_7 \ \land\ a^{\mathrm{lo}}\ge b^{\mathrm{lo}}\big)
          \iff (A_7\wedge\lnot B_7)\ \lor\ \big((A_7=B_7)\wedge L\big).
\]
In bit-$7$ algebra, $a_7\wedge\lnot b_7$ realises $A_7\wedge\lnot B_7$;
$\lnot(a_7\oplus b_7)$ has bit $7$ equal to $[A_7=B_7]$, and conjoining with $g_0$
(whose bit $7$ is $L$) realises $(A_7=B_7)\wedge L$. Their disjunction is exactly the
predicate above, and the final $\wedge\,\mathrm{HI}$ keeps only the per-lane verdict
bits.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Per-lane mask expansion]\label{lem:expand}
Let $g$ have support in $\mathrm{HI}$ (only bit $8k+7$ may be set per lane). Then
$e = (g \wsub (g\gg 7))\,|\,g$ equals $\code{0xFF}$ in each lane whose top bit is set
and $\code{0x00}$ otherwise, with no cross-lane interaction.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Logical $\gg 7$ maps bit $8k+7$ to bit $8k$ (the low bit of the \emph{same} lane $k$),
so $g\gg 7$ holds $\code{0x01}$ in exactly the selected lanes. Within a selected lane,
$\code{0x80}\wsub\code{0x01}=\code{0x7F}$ and the minuend dominates, so no borrow
leaves the lane; an unselected lane gives $0\wsub 0=0$. Finally OR-ing $g$ restores the
top bit: $\code{0x7F}\,|\,\code{0x80}=\code{0xFF}$ and $0\,|\,0=0$.
\end{proof}

Combining Theorem~\ref{thm:ge} and Lemma~\ref{lem:expand} gives the corrected,
verified primitive now in the library:
\begin{lstlisting}
fn swar_byte_ge_mask(a: u64, b: u64) -> u64 {
    const HI: u64 = 0x8080_8080_8080_8080;
    const LO: u64 = 0x7F7F_7F7F_7F7F_7F7F;
    let a7 = a & HI; let b7 = b & HI;
    let borrow = (a | HI).wrapping_sub(b & LO) & HI;     // Lemma 4.1
    let ge = ((a7 & !b7) | (!(a7 ^ b7) & borrow)) & HI;  // Theorem 4.2
    ge.wrapping_sub(ge >> 7) | ge                        // Lemma 4.3: 0xFF per lane
}
fn swar_byte_max(a: u64, b: u64) -> u64 {
    let m = swar_byte_ge_mask(a, b);
    (a & m) | (b & !m)
}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Tournament reduction}
\begin{theorem}[Horizontal maximum]\label{thm:tournament}
Let $\oslash(x,y)$ be the per-lane max of Theorem~\ref{thm:ge}/Lemma~\ref{lem:expand}.
Then
\[
  v_1=\oslash(v, v\gg 8),\quad v_2=\oslash(v_1, v_1\gg 16),\quad v_3=\oslash(v_2, v_2\gg 32)
\]
places $\max_k v_k$ in lane $0$ of $v_3$; multiplying by
$\code{0x0101\_0101\_0101\_0101}$ broadcasts it to all eight lanes.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
By induction on rounds. After round $1$, lane $k$ holds $\max(v_k, v_{k+1})$, i.e.\ the
max over a window of width $2$. The shift by $16$ then by $32$ doubles the window each
round, so after three rounds lane $0$ holds the max over a window of width
$8$ --- all lanes. Each $\oslash$ is correct per Theorem~\ref{thm:ge}. The final
multiply by the all-ones-byte constant is the standard SWAR broadcast.
\end{proof}
The minimum is identical with the selection arguments of $\oslash$ swapped
(Corollary~\ref{cor:compl} again: where $a\ge b$, $b$ is the minimum).

\section{The null-byte primitive}
The classic SWAR test for a zero byte \cite{anderson,hackersdelight},
$(x \wsub \code{0x0101\dots01}) \wedge \lnot x \wedge \code{0x8080\dots80}$, is nonzero
iff some lane of $x$ is zero; it underlies fast string scanning. It is included here as
the archetype of carry-isolated SWAR: the subtraction borrows into a lane's top bit
exactly when that lane was zero, and $\lnot x \wedge \mathrm{HI}$ keeps the signal only
where the original top bit was clear.

\chapter{The Limits of ``Branchless''}

This chapter is the honest counterweight to the proofs. A correctness proof over
$\mathbb{W}_w$ establishes \emph{functional} correctness and a fixed control-flow graph
\emph{at the source level}. Neither property is automatically preserved to the metal.

\section{Source code is not machine code}
The Rust source contains no branch, but the compiler is free to introduce one (e.g.\
lowering a \code{clamp} to a compare-and-jump it deems cheaper) or, conversely, to
lower an explicit \code{if} to a branchless \code{CMOV}. The library leans on
\code{\#[inline(always)]} and a mask-only formulation to keep the optimiser on the
branchless path, but \emph{the only authority is the emitted assembly}. Verifying the
branchless property therefore requires inspecting machine code (or using a checker such
as \texttt{ct-verif}/\texttt{dudect} \cite{ctverif,dudect}), not reading the Rust.

\section{CMOV and data-independent latency}
A conditional move executes in fixed time independent of its condition on the
microarchitectures of interest, which is exactly what we want. But ``branchless''
$\neq$ ``\code{CMOV}''; some branchless idioms lower to longer dependency chains, and
the latency-vs-throughput profile differs across cores \cite{fog}. The measured numbers
in Chapter~6 are therefore reported for a specific build and machine, not as universal
constants.

\section{Data-dependent instruction latency}
Some instructions are not constant-time even though they are branch-free. Integer
division is the salient example: the library's \code{adler32} computes a modulo by the
prime $65521$, which lowers to a hardware divide whose latency is operand-dependent on
several microarchitectures (early-terminating dividers). Calling such a routine
``branchless'' is accurate; calling it ``constant-time'' in the security sense is
\emph{not}. We document the distinction at the call site rather than papering over it.

\section{Approximation versus exactness}
Branchlessness sometimes forces approximation. The library's fixed-point sine uses the
Bhaskara~I rational approximation \cite{bhaskara}, valid on $[0^\circ,180^\circ]$ and
extended to the full circle by quadrant folding. It is branchless and fast but
\emph{approximate}: measured error against a double-precision oracle is bounded by
about $1300$ Q16 units ($\approx 0.02$). A correctness ``proof'' for such a routine is a
proof of an \emph{error bound}, not of equality --- a different and weaker guarantee
that must be stated as such.

\section{What constant-time security actually requires}
Operand-independent \emph{instruction sequence} is necessary but not sufficient for
constant-time security. One also needs operand-independent \emph{memory access
patterns} (no secret-dependent table indices or cache footprints) and
operand-independent instruction \emph{latencies} (ruling out the division case above).
The mask calculus delivers the first; the other two are properties of the surrounding
code and the target ISA. This thesis proves the algebra, not the deployment.

\chapter{Empirical Evaluation and Verification Audit}

\section{Methodology}
All timings use the Criterion statistical harness \cite{criterion}: a warm-up phase
followed by $100$ samples per configuration, with outlier detection and a reported
confidence interval. Every measured input and result is passed through
\code{std::hint::black\_box} to defeat the optimiser.

\begin{remark}[Why trivial inputs lie]
A benchmark that calls a pure function on a compile-time-constant argument and discards
the result measures \emph{nothing}: LLVM constant-folds the call away, and the loop
becomes empty. An early version of this project's benchmarks did exactly this and
reported implausibly low times. The fix --- wrap inputs and outputs in
\code{black\_box} and use representative, non-constant data --- is what makes the
numbers below meaningful. This is a methodological prerequisite, not a detail.
\end{remark}

\section{Verification audit: the gap between claimed and actual correctness}
The most important empirical result of this thesis is negative.

\paragraph{Defect.} The SWAR byte-wise maximum (Chapter~4) was implemented with the
low-seven-bit comparison of Section~4.3. A differential test against the obvious scalar
reference (extract eight lanes, take the max, broadcast) reveals disagreement on
$142{,}189$ of $200{,}000$ uniformly random $64$-bit inputs ($71.1\%$), and on a
concrete witness $\code{0x85}$ vs $\code{0x03}$ it returns $\code{0x05}$ instead of
$\code{0x85}$.

\paragraph{Why the tests missed it.} Every unit test for the routine used byte values
below $\code{0x80}$ (e.g.\ the vector $[3,1,4,1,5,9,2,6]$) or the uniform cases $0$ and
$\code{0xFF}$. None mixed a high-bit byte with a smaller one, which is the only regime
where the discarded top bit matters. There was no randomized oracle. The curated
examples were, in effect, chosen to pass.

\paragraph{Fix and re-verification.} We derived the corrected comparison of
Theorem~\ref{thm:ge} with the expansion of Lemma~\ref{lem:expand}. The replacement
agrees with the scalar reference on \emph{all} $65{,}536$ two-byte combinations
(exhaustive) and on $500{,}000$ random $64$-bit words ($0$ mismatches), and the library
now carries a $200{,}000$-iteration randomized oracle test (a self-contained
\code{xorshift64} stream) plus a high-bit doctest, so the defect cannot regress.

\paragraph{Stubs named honestly.} The audit also confirms that several ``verification''
artefacts in the wider codebase --- the \code{*\_phd\_gate} functions and
\code{swar\_mask\_ones} --- are \emph{identity functions}. They carry comments implying
formal verification but compute nothing; they are scaffolding, and we decline to count
them as evidence of anything.

\section{Lesson}
Example-based tests demonstrate the presence of behaviour on the chosen examples; they
cannot demonstrate its absence elsewhere. For a small, total, integer-valued kernel the
right standard is \emph{differential testing against an independent reference over a
large or exhaustive input space}, ideally backed by the algebraic proofs of
Chapters~2--4. Where the input space is too large to exhaust ($2^{64}$ here), a fixed
randomized stream with a known seed gives reproducible, high-coverage evidence at
negligible cost. The proofs tell us \emph{why} the kernel is correct; the differential
oracle catches the moment the code stops matching the proof.

\chapter{From Kernels to Autonomic Control Planes}

The preceding chapters establish the $\mathcal{B}$-calculus as a correct
foundation for integer kernels. This chapter traces how that foundation propagates
upward: through a branchless Petri-net substrate to a self-managing process
engine --- and, more carefully, what this enables at scale and what it still does
not guarantee.

\section{Context: why sub-microsecond primitives matter at system scale}

A single branchless \code{select\_u32} at $1.71\,\mathrm{ns}$ is not
interesting in isolation. It becomes interesting when it is the innermost
operation inside a tight loop that runs across millions of events per second on
hundreds of nodes. At that point the arithmetic cost of a branch-misprediction
($15$--$20$ cycles, $\approx 5$--$7\,\mathrm{ns}$ at $3\,\mathrm{GHz}$) is not
paid once but billions of times per second across the fleet. The compound effect
is not a constant additive penalty: on cache-cold, high-entropy workloads ---
financial matching engines, real-time telemetry ingestion, distributed consensus
protocols --- the misprediction rate can reach $30$--$50\%$ of dynamic branches,
which turns a $5\,\mathrm{ns}$ primitive into a $10\,\mathrm{ns}$ one. That
$2\times$ slowdown collapses throughput headroom that the system designer expected
to spend on application logic.

The inverse is equally real: eliminating data-dependent branches from the hot
path of a control plane not only recovers cycles but also makes the worst-case
execution time (WCET) \emph{statically bounded}. Hard real-time schedulers
(aerospace flight computers, industrial PLCs, financial exchange matching) require
a bound on the worst case, not the average; branchless kernels collapse the
distribution to a narrow spike, which is precisely what makes the bound tight.

\section{The branchless Petri-net substrate}

The library implements a Petri-net execution substrate in
\code{crates/bcinr-logic/src/models/petri.rs}. A Petri net is a bipartite
directed graph of \emph{places} (holding tokens) and \emph{transitions} (firing
rules). A transition fires when every input place is marked; firing consumes the
input tokens and produces output tokens. Petri nets are a classical model for
concurrent, reactive, and workflow-style systems \cite{murata}.

The key representation decision is to encode the token distribution as a
word-aligned bitset: place $k$ maps to bit $k$ of a \code{KBitSet<WORDS>} whose
underlying storage is a \code{[u64; WORDS]} array. This lets enabledness checking
and firing become word-parallel bitwise operations on $64$ places per
\code{u64} word.

\begin{definition}[KBitSet]
\code{KBitSet<WORDS>} is the type $\{0,1\}^{64 \cdot \text{WORDS}}$ with the
following primitives (all CC$=1$):
\[
  \code{set}(k):\ \text{words}[k\gg 6] \,\mathrel{|}=\, 1 \ll (k \wedge 63),
\]
\[
  \code{contains}(k):\ (\text{words}[k\gg 6] \gg (k \wedge 63)) \wedge 1 = 1,
\]
\[
  \code{satisfies}(R):\ \bigvee_{i} (R.\text{words}[i] \wedge \lnot \text{self.words}[i]) = \mathbf{0}.
\]
All three are constant-time in the length of \code{WORDS}; there are no
data-dependent branches.
\end{definition}

The firing rule in \code{SwarMarking::try\_fire} combines these with the
$\select$ operator of Chapter~2:

\begin{lstlisting}
pub fn try_fire(&self, input: KBitSet<W>, output: KBitSet<W>)
    -> (Self, bool)
{
    let is_enabled = self.current.satisfies(input);
    let mask = 0u64.wrapping_sub(is_enabled as u64); // 0 or 0xFFFF...
    let mut next = KBitSet::<W>::zero();
    (0..W).for_each(|i| {
        let fired  = (self.current.words[i] & !input.words[i]) | output.words[i];
        next.words[i] = (fired & mask) | (self.current.words[i] & !mask);
    });
    (Self { current: next }, is_enabled)
}
\end{lstlisting}

The pattern \code{0u64.wrapping\_sub(flag as u64)} is precisely the less-than
mask of Theorem~\ref{thm:lt} applied to a boolean: it broadcasts the
$\{0,1\}$ enabledness bit to an all-ones or all-zeros word, and the subsequent
word-parallel select is Theorem~\ref{thm:select} applied word-by-word. The
Petri-net layer is therefore not an independent design; it is a direct application
of the mask calculus.

\begin{theorem}[Branchless firing correctness]\label{thm:fire}
Let $m$ be a marking and let $T=(I,O)$ be a transition with input set $I$ and
output set $O$. Define $e = m.\code{satisfies}(I)$. Then
$\code{try\_fire}(I,O)$ returns the marking
\[
  m' = \begin{cases}
    (m \setminus I) \cup O & \text{if } e, \\
    m                      & \text{if } \lnot e,
  \end{cases}
\]
and the flag $e$, with no conditional branches.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
When $e=\mathtt{true}$, \code{mask}$=\mathbf{1}$, so
$\code{next.words}[i]=((\text{self.words}[i] \wedge \lnot I.\text{words}[i])|O.\text{words}[i])
\wedge\mathbf{1} \,|\, \text{self.words}[i]\wedge\mathbf{0}
= (m\setminus I)\cup O$.
When $e=\mathtt{false}$, \code{mask}$=\mathbf{0}$, so
$\code{next.words}[i]=\mathbf{0}\,|\,\text{self.words}[i]=m$.
The control-flow graph has no branches; only bitwise and wrapping arithmetic.
\end{proof}

\section{The Vision 2030 autonomic engine}

\code{Vision2030Engine} in \code{src/models/vision\_2030.rs} builds a
self-managing (autonomic) process engine on top of the Petri-net substrate. It
implements the MAPE-K loop \cite{kephart} --- Monitor, Analyze, Plan, Execute ---
in a fully branchless execution core:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Phase} & \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Mechanism} \\
\midrule
Monitor   & \code{observe}  & FNV-1a hash lookup in \code{PackedKeyTable} \\
Analyze   & \code{infer}    & Read \code{AutonomicState} (integrity, throughput, health) \\
Plan      & \code{propose}  & Return \code{Vec<AutonomicAction>} based on state flags \\
Execute   & \code{execute}  & Branchless reset via mask select on marking \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The \code{observe} method hashes an incoming activity label (e.g.\ ``Process'')
with FNV-1a, looks it up in a \code{PackedKeyTable<u64, u8, 16>} (a packed
hash table with CC$=1$ lookup), and fires the corresponding Petri-net transition
via \code{SwarMarking::try\_fire}. The resulting marking update and state
adjustment (integrity, drift detection) use only the mask arithmetic of
Chapters~2--4; the method contains no \code{if} or \code{match} in its hot path.

The engine is parameterised over \code{WORDS} at compile time, so the net can
scale from a 3-place toy (\code{WORDS=1}) to a 256-place industrial process
(\code{WORDS=4}) without changing the algorithm.

\section{Civilization-scale unlocks: what is actually claimed}

It would be easy to reach for large language here --- ``branchless control planes
enable civilization-scale coordination'' --- and the library's earlier documentation
did exactly that. This section states the honest claim instead.

\paragraph{What the calculus genuinely enables.}
The $\mathcal{B}$-calculus and the Petri-net substrate give three concrete
engineering guarantees that compound at scale:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Statically bounded latency per transition.} A single
  \code{try\_fire} on a \code{KBitSet<4>} (256 places) costs exactly
  $4 \times 3 = 12$ bitwise-arithmetic operations plus a hash lookup --- a
  constant independent of the current marking, the number of tokens, or the
  activity label. This is the WCET property, and it is the property that makes
  the engine composable into a hard real-time control loop.

  \item \textbf{Deterministic failure behaviour.} An unenabled transition returns
  the unchanged marking and \code{fired=false} in the same time as a successful
  fire. There is no ``slow path'' for the error case, no exception, and no heap
  allocation on failure. Systems that must meet SLAs under adversarial input
  (e.g.\ a transaction processing engine under DoS) need this property.

  \item \textbf{Side-channel resistance at the transition layer.} The Petri-net
  firing rule does not branch on the marking values, so an observer watching only
  the instruction stream cannot infer \emph{which} transition fired from timing
  alone. This is weaker than full constant-time security (Chapter~5 still applies:
  the FNV-1a lookup touches a table, and the hash is not secret), but it closes
  the most obvious timing oracle at the decision layer.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What the calculus does not provide.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Correctness of the process model.} The firing rule is correct
  (Theorem~\ref{thm:fire}), but whether the Petri-net topology correctly encodes
  the intended business process is a modelling question outside the algebra. A
  branchless incorrect process model is still incorrect.
  \item \textbf{Distributed consistency.} Nothing in this chapter addresses
  multi-node consensus, network partitions, or replication. A control plane
  composed of branchless kernels is fast and predictable locally; whether it
  behaves correctly across a distributed fleet depends on the protocols layered
  above it.
  \item \textbf{Security of the activity-label channel.} The \code{observe}
  method trusts the \code{payload} byte string. An adversary who can inject
  arbitrary payloads can fire arbitrary transitions. The mask calculus prevents
  timing leakage of the firing outcome; it does not authenticate the input.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{A grounded characterisation.}
The honest claim is: \textit{the $\mathcal{B}$-calculus and \code{Vision2030Engine}
provide a formally grounded, WCET-bounded, branch-free substrate for autonomic
process management, suited to environments where latency predictability and
side-channel hygiene at the decision layer are requirements}. That is a real and
useful guarantee. Whether it amounts to a ``civilization-scale unlock'' depends
entirely on whether such environments matter at civilization scale --- a question
this thesis does not answer.

\section{Verification status of the application layer}

The application layer introduces two components not covered by Chapters~2--5:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \code{PackedKeyTable}: a compact hash table with open addressing.
  The \code{lookup} method is CC$=1$ by construction (no branch on hash
  collision; it uses linear probing with a fixed-length loop). This claim is
  verified by the \code{bcinr-contract-gate} tool, which checks cyclomatic
  complexity.

  \item \code{Vision2030Engine::observe}: the method's hot path is branchless
  (mask-based select on \code{valid\_idx}), but the \code{is\_some()} call on
  \code{PackedKeyTable::get} is a boolean extracted to a branchless integer
  via the \code{as usize} cast. The resulting code compiles to a conditional
  move on x86-64 (verified by inspecting \code{cargo objdump} output), not a
  branch.
\end{enumerate}

Both components carry the same adversarial differential tests as the core
primitives: three mutant functions that invert, increment, or XOR the reference
output, rejected by the unit tests in \code{vision\_2030.rs} and
\code{kernel.rs}.

\paragraph{Remaining gaps.} The \code{adapt} and \code{manifest} methods contain
floating-point arithmetic (\code{f32} clamping) that is not covered by the integer
calculus of Chapters~2--4. Floating-point \code{clamp} lowers to
\code{MAXSS}/\code{MINSS} on x86, which is branchless --- but the latency is
not guaranteed constant across denormals and NaN inputs on all microarchitectures.
For the current \code{[0.0, 1.0]} range this is not a practical concern, but it
is a gap in the formal coverage.

\chapter{Conclusion}

The defensible core of \textit{bcinr} is small and genuinely solid: a mask calculus
whose single operator $\select$ is provably correct (Theorem~\ref{thm:select}), whose
mask generators are provably correct (Theorems~\ref{thm:lt}--\ref{thm:eq}), and from
which \code{min}, \code{max}, \code{abs}, \code{clamp}, saturating arithmetic, and
carry-isolated SWAR reductions follow by composition with proofs
(Chapters~3--4). Measured latencies confirm the constant-time, sub-$10\,\mathrm{ns}$
intent for the scalar primitives, and hash throughputs land where algorithmic structure
predicts.

Chapter~7 traces the calculus upward: the same $\select$ primitive underlies the
branchless Petri-net transition rule (Theorem~\ref{thm:fire}), which in turn is the
execution core of the \code{Vision2030Engine} autonomic process manager. This vertical
coherence --- from a two-instruction mask generator to a self-managing control plane ---
is the architectural claim. It is a real and concrete claim. The chapter is equally
explicit about what it does not claim: correctness of the process model, distributed
consistency, and input authentication are out of scope.

The honest findings matter as much as the proofs. ``Branchless'' is a source-level
property the compiler may not preserve; ``constant-time'' in the security sense demands
operand-independent memory and latency that this algebra alone does not provide; and a
``verified'' routine can still be wrong when its tests are curated rather than
adversarial --- as the SWAR maximum was, on $71\%$ of inputs, until this work corrected
it. The contribution is therefore not a sweeping claim but a disciplined one: a proven
calculus, measured faithfully, audited adversarially, applied to a concrete autonomic
substrate, with its limits stated plainly at every level.

\paragraph{Future work.} Four directions would strengthen the guarantees from
\emph{argued} to \emph{checked}: (i) machine-checked proofs of Chapters~2--4 in a proof
assistant or SMT-backed verifier (e.g.\ Why3); (ii) constant-time verification of the
emitted machine code with \texttt{ct-verif} or statistical leakage testing with
\texttt{dudect}; (iii) replacing the remaining identity-stub ``gates'' with either
real verification anchors or nothing at all; and (iv) extending the integer calculus
to cover the floating-point \code{adapt}/\code{manifest} paths in
\code{Vision2030Engine}, closing the formal coverage gap identified in Section~7.5.
